Dot patterns

The dots pattern

The dots are Rhapsody's primary graphic device, and they carry the meaning of what we do. Each dot is a data point, a system, a person. Rhapsody's work is to connect that data, clean it, and organize it, and the dots tell that story: many separate points brought into alignment, resolving from scattered noise into an ordered, legible field. Used well, they signal connectivity, precision, and scale while adding texture and refinement.

How the dots work

Pick a background, a color mode, and the composition. The main composition is the uniform scattered field; at small sizes the big dot (one oversized dot resolving into a few) carries the same story; and dots can also be composed into framing and diagram elements. All are covered below. Whatever you choose, the dots stay to a corner or edge, cover no more than 50 percent of the space, and never overlap text.

Dot field or big dot?

Either device, never both in one layout, and the framing and diagram elements below are a third, more decorative use.

Backgrounds and dot colors

Each field is built for one background and is not interchangeable. The background sets which dot colors are allowed:

Background Dot colors
Navy #0B2C47 White, Rhapsody blue, light blue, teal, purple, plus a maximum of 3 orange.
Rhapsody blue #1A81F4 White only.
White #FFFFFF Navy, Rhapsody blue, light blue, teal, purple, plus a maximum of 3 orange.

Orange lives in multicolor fields only, never on a Rhapsody blue ground, and never more than 3 dots so it reads as a surprise. The field is exempt from the three-color rule.

The dot field

The dot field is structured dispersion: a cohesive cluster of identical dots on one invisible grid that gathers into a dense core at an edge or corner and dissolves unevenly outward into a transition zone and a few connected outliers. Energetic and fluid, but intelligently organized, randomized, never random. It never forms a recognizable object, symbol, letter, line, wave, or geometric shape. (The older graduated field, where dot size changed across the grid, is retired.)

A closed, approved family, use it, don’t build it

There are 45 approved master compositions: 3 backgrounds × 3 anchors × 5 formats, each as a transparent SVG and a 2× PNG. Pick the file that fits and use it as-is, flipped, or rotated 90°. Do not hand-place a new field, and never restyle one, no recoloring, resizing individual dots, gradients, opacity, strokes, or non-proportional stretching. Hand-built fields drift off the grid and off-brand.

The three anchors

The anchor is where the field’s centre of gravity sits. Keep all copy in the clear area opposite it.

Formats

Five formats cover the common canvases: 16×9 (slides, web hero, video), 1×1 (social), 4×5 (social portrait), 9×16 (story, vertical video), and a 2560×720 banner (email header, web banner).

Use the approved masters (transparent SVG preferred, 2× PNG fallback), verbatim or flipped/rotated, rather than building fields by hand: Scattered Dots.

The rules for the dot field

Clearance is the rule most often broken. No copy, headline, button, card, logo, or image sits on top of a field, and nothing crops one. Leave at least one full grid pitch between the outermost dot and any other object (about 87px at 1920×1080, scaling with the pitch at other sizes). Cover no more than 50 percent of the space, keep copy in the clear area opposite the anchor, and use one field per layout, never two fields, and never a field with the big dot.

Building an on-brand variation

Prefer the masters. If a format outside the family is ever needed, these measured values define a valid field:

  • Grid: pitch = long edge ÷ 22; every dot identical, diameter 38.9% of the pitch, always on-grid (an 87.3px pitch with 34px dots at 1920×1080).
  • Density: 17–25% of grid cells filled, densest along the anchor edge and thinning outward.
  • One connected mass: no empty interior lines, no detached dots or clusters, no thin protruding arms; the longest straight run is 4 dots in any direction.
  • Flat fill only: perfect circles, no gradient, opacity, or stroke; max 3 orange in multicolor fields, never on Rhapsody blue.
Approval checklist

A field is on-brand only if every answer is yes: one clear centre of gravity; reads as a single connected field; a gradual dense-to-sparse transition; all dots the same size on one grid; an uneven but not chaotic outer contour; outliers visually connected to the mass; generous, intentional open space; stays abstract from a distance; no long lines, stair-steps, shapes, or repeated sequences; and, in multicolor, balanced colors with no secondary pattern.

The big dot

At a large scale, a single dot becomes a hero graphic: one oversized brand-color circle bleeding off a corner or edge, with two to four smaller dots trailing from it. It reads as one data point resolving into many, and it carries the dot story where a full scattered field would be too busy or too small to resolve, display ads, social tiles, thin banners, and cover slides. It is the dot motif’s compact, high-impact form, and the preferred device for small and fast-read formats.

It carries a specific meaning. Where the scattered field shows scale and order, many equal points resolving from noise into an aligned whole, the big dot shows relationship and momentum. One dominant dot with smaller dots gathering and overlapping around it reads as data sources and organizations of every size coming into connection: a large health system, a lab, a clinic, an app, each a different scale, drawn into one connected network with Rhapsody as the anchor. The overlap is the point, separate systems no longer siloed but touching and exchanging. It is the warmer, more human counterpart to the precise grid, which is why it leads brand and hero moments, and it maps to the go-to-market story, one platform others build and connect around across both pillars (Build the infrastructure, Automate the work).

Framing and diagram elements

Beyond backgrounds, the dots can be composed into shapes, a ring, an arc, a network, or a corner cluster, that frame content or carry a simple idea. Use them as decorative and structural accents in creative work: digital display ads, white papers and reports, banners, and slides. Here the dots are always equal size, always multicolor but never orange, and carry no gradient fill. A faint connecting line is allowed for a diagram, never a squiggle. Coverage stays within 50 percent and clear of text.

At a glance: do and don't

On-brand
  • Uniform scattered field (equal-size dots) on white, blue, or navy.
  • Single color or multicolor; multicolor includes one to five orange dots. Exempt from the three-color rule.
  • Anchored to a corner or edge, no more than 50 percent, with a clear gap from text.
  • The one dot device in the layout.
Off-brand
  • Full-view takeover or more than 50 percent coverage.
  • Random placement, mixed sizes, or dots over text, faces, or the logo.
  • Off-palette, outlined, or gradient-filled dots.
  • Low-contrast pairings, or a solid block that never dissolves.

Dot row accent

A short horizontal row of dots is an approved compact use of the dot motif, useful in banners and email headers where a full dot field would be too much.

  • A few dots, one uniform size, evenly spaced.
  • Sequence through brand colors, for example teal, blue, light blue, purple.
  • Use as a small accent, not a full field. Do not combine with a dot field in the same layout.
