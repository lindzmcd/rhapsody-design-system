Business card

A two-sided card that does one job per face. The front is the brand moment: navy, the big-dot device, the logo, and the person. The back is the reason the card still exists in 2026 — two QR codes that put you in someone’s LinkedIn or their inbox before the conversation cools off. Nothing on the card asks to be typed out.

The idea

Hand over a card, they scan it, you’re connected before you’ve finished shaking hands

The card

Shown close to actual size, scaling with the window. Trim is the US standard 3.5 × 2 in; the same layout holds at 85 × 55 mm for the European card. The drop shadow and the hairline around the white face are here so the cards read against the page — neither is printed.

Front

  • Ground. Rhapsody Navy, full bleed. The card is the one place the brand gets a whole surface to itself, so don’t dilute it with a second panel or a rule.
  • Device. One big-dot ecosystem master (navy register, variant B) placed as-is and cropped into the bottom-right corner — blue anchor, white, light-blue and teal companions. It is scaled and cropped to the format, never rebuilt or recolored. This is the card’s only graphic device: no Aurora, no fluid dots, no photo.
  • Logo. One-color white, 1.5 in wide, top-left — the print minimum, and the safe choice at this size. The blue-wordmark-and-white-line lockup is for larger formats. Clear space equal to the height of the R is kept on every side; the device is placed so the nearest dot clears it by a further 0.2 in.
  • Name lockup. Name in Poppins Medium (500) white at 10 pt, title in Regular (400) light blue at 7.2 pt, sitting on the baseline of the card. Order is always name then title.

Back

  • Ground. White. QR codes need a light ground and a quiet zone to scan, so this face stays clean — no device, no tint, no bleed color.
  • Two codes, two jobs. Left opens the person’s LinkedIn profile, right opens a pre-addressed email. Labelling them is the whole point: an unlabelled QR is a coin toss, and people scan the one they actually want.
  • Labels. The teal tick — a short teal bar before a navy mono label — is how teal is allowed to appear on a light ground. The label is navy, never teal.
  • One contact stack, right-aligned. Phone, then email, then the website actionlink, set as a single block at the foot. Keeping the three in one stack is what lets a reader find the one they want.
  • Written fallback. Phone, email and rhapsody.health → stay in type for anyone who would rather type than scan, and so the card still works in a photo, a scanner app, or a country where the QR lands on a blocked domain.
  • No logo, and no name. The front carries both. Repeating the wordmark on the back is the most common way this card goes wrong, and repeating the name is what breaks it: a long name beside a long email address will not fit the safe width, and the two run into the trim edge. The back identifies the person through the email address and the codes.

The QR codes

Generated by assets/qr/make-card-qr.py, which writes print-ready SVG for any employee:

python3 make-card-qr.py "Michelle Blackmer" Michelle.Blackmer@rhapsody.health michelle-blackmer
  • Modules are round and tangent, so the code reads as part of the brand’s dot language rather than a generic black square. Finder eyes are softened to match.
  • Color is Rhapsody Navy on white, not Rhapsody Blue. Blue on white is about 3:1 — fine for type, too low for a camera to binarize reliably. Navy is the brand-correct ink and the one that scans.
  • Size is 0.85 in square, which puts each module at 0.65 mm — comfortably above the 0.6 mm floor for phone cameras. The generator picks the strongest error-correction level that still clears that floor and prints the figure, so a longer URL never silently shrinks the modules below it.
  • Quiet zone. Two modules are drawn into the file; the tile padding on the card supplies the rest. Never crop into it or set type inside it.
  • A square-module version of every code is written alongside as -square.svg. It is the fallback if a print run ever scans poorly — swap it in rather than enlarging or recoloring the rounded one.

Every code was decode-tested before shipping, with zxing-cpp and OpenCV, across resolutions from 600 px down to 110 px and with blur applied. The rounded codes read in every condition. Rounded modules with visible gaps between them did not — they fail on weaker scanner apps — which is why the modules are tangent and why the radius is not a style knob.

Safe area

Everything on both faces holds 0.229 in from trim, against a declared safe area of 0.22 in. That figure is worth re-measuring rather than trusting by eye whenever copy changes: an earlier draft of this card repeated the name on the back, and the longest name in the pair pushed the email address out to 0.136 in from the edge while the shorter name looked perfectly comfortable. Nothing on the back is set to wrap, so an overlong line pushes into the trim rather than reflowing where you can see it.

Print production

  • Trim 3.5 × 2 in, bleed 0.125 in on all four sides, safe area 0.22 in in from trim. The navy and the device bleed; nothing else approaches the edge.
  • Color. Set Rhapsody Blue explicitly as CMYK 100 50 0 0 rather than letting the layout program convert the hex, which comes out muddy. Navy, teal and light blue use their CMYK equivalents.
  • Stock. 16–18 pt uncoated or soft-touch. Avoid gloss: it throws camera flare straight back at the QR codes and costs you scans.
  • Codes must be vector in the final artwork. Never place a rasterized QR, and never let a printer “optimize” or re-trace it.
  • Proof by scanning. Scan both codes off the physical proof, in ordinary indoor light, on an iPhone and an Android, before the run is approved.

Options

  • Phone number. Included. Drop the line for roles that would rather not print a direct number; the layout closes up and the two codes still carry the card.
  • One code instead of two. A single vCard QR saves the whole contact in one scan but drops them nowhere — no profile to look at, no draft to send. Use it only for roles that hand out cards in volume at events.
  • Rounded die-cut. A squared-top, rounded-bottom die would echo the system’s corner rule in the physical object. It is a custom die and a longer lead time; the standard square cut is the default.

The NFC option

An NFC inlay is a paper-thin antenna laminated inside the card. Hold an unlocked phone against it and a notification opens whatever the tag points at, with no app and no camera. It is the one upgrade that makes a card feel genuinely modern, and it carries a constraint worth understanding before anyone orders a run.

  • A tag holds one destination. This is the part that catches people out. NFC cannot offer a choice, so it can't do what the two codes do. Pointing it at a vCard saves the contact but shows them nothing; pointing it at LinkedIn drops the email. To match this card, the tap needs a per-person profile page carrying both actions — which means that page has to exist and be maintained. That is a web project, not a print decision, so settle it first.
  • It does not replace the printed codes. Recent iPhones and most Android phones read tags with the screen on and unlocked, but older handsets need the reader opened by hand, and a tap that doesn't register fails silently with nothing on the card to fall back to. The QR codes stay exactly as they are.
  • Lock the tags before they go out. An unlocked NFC tag can be rewritten by anyone holding a phone to it. Write-protect them as part of production and treat that as a requirement, not a nicety.
  • Production is a different supply chain. Not every card printer does NFC; it needs an inlay-capable supplier, constrains the stock, and carries a higher unit cost and a minimum order. Get a quote before committing — the economics usually decide this on their own.
  • Where it earns its place. Executives and the event team, where the card is handed over in volume and the tap is part of the impression. The standard card stays as drawn.
Do
  • Label every code with what it opens
  • Keep the QR navy on white, at 0.85 in or larger
  • Regenerate codes with the script when someone’s details change
  • Leave the back free of the logo and of any graphic device
  • Scan the physical proof before approving the run
Don’t
  • Put the logo, a dot device or Aurora on the QR face
  • Recolor a code to Rhapsody Blue, teal, or white-on-navy
  • Drop a logo into the middle of a code, or crop its quiet zone
  • Shrink a code below 0.85 in to win back space — cut copy instead
  • Add the name or title back to the QR face to fill the space
  • Hand a printer a JPG or PNG of a code

Before this goes to print

  • Swap REPLACE-HANDLE for each person’s real LinkedIn vanity URL and regenerate — the codes shown here point at a placeholder path on purpose.
  • Confirm each title against the directory; titles are never abbreviated to fit.
  • Check the email in type matches the one encoded in the code.
  • Replace both placeholder phone numbers with real direct lines, in the same +1 555 012 3456 spacing, and confirm each person wants theirs printed.
