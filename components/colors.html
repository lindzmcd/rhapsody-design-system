Color

Rhapsody Blue is the primary color, used most prominently. Rhapsody Navy, light blue, and teal are secondary. Purple and orange are restricted tertiary accents, not general-purpose layout colors. A standard Rhapsody layout should usually contain no tertiary colors at all. Full-page backgrounds stay white, Rhapsody Blue, or Rhapsody Navy; light gray and gray-blue are for content modules, not full-page fills.

Text color

Body copy uses only white, Rhapsody Navy, or black. Work down the list in order.

  • Rhapsody Navy is the default text color. On light backgrounds, set body copy and headings in Rhapsody Navy (#0B2C47) rather than black. Navy is the on-brand dark and clears contrast on white easily.
  • Black is a fallback, not a default. Reach for black (#050F19) only when navy fails a contrast requirement, or in print that can’t reproduce navy (single-color or limited-ink jobs). Otherwise, navy.
  • Rhapsody Blue is the accent on light. Use it for emphasis, links, and one accent word in a headline, not for running body copy.
  • On dark backgrounds, text is white. For blue accents on navy, the two blues split by size—see “Rhapsody Blue on navy” below.
  • No tertiary color in type: teal, purple, and orange are never used for body copy, headings, eyebrows, or labels. Two exceptions: on navy, teal may set an eyebrow, label, small icon, or actionlink (6.7:1 on navy — see Teal in small applications below), and an H3 may take a secondary color where contrast allows (navy on light; light blue or teal on dark). On light grounds there is no teal type of any kind: automation actionlinks stay Rhapsody Blue, and the teal cue is carried by a nearby accent (module accent line, tick, seam). H1, H2, body copy, and captions stay within the copy colors above.
  • Dark gray for supporting text. Dark gray (#7E858C) is allowed for secondary/supporting copy on white—subheads, support lines, captions—where a softer, de-emphasized read is wanted. Body copy still stays navy or black.
  • Color spaces: digital uses HEX or RGB; print uses CMYK or Pantone.
Default palette hierarchy

Most layouts should use White/Navy + Rhapsody Blue, with one optional secondary accent (Light Blue or Teal) only when needed. No more than three brand colors per layout, and reaching the maximum is not a goal. Purple and Orange do not count as freely available third colors; they appear only where another rule explicitly authorizes them. The multicolor dot field and data visualizations remain controlled exceptions.

No custom gradients

Brand color is applied flat. Don’t create or add gradients—no color-to-color blends, ramps, or fades in backgrounds, fills, type, or shapes. The one approved exception is the soft glow of the Aurora light device (see Light devices); reach for it anywhere a gradient might feel warranted, and nowhere else.

Color accessibility

Accessibility requirements depend on the type of content. Use the thresholds below and then apply the brand-usage rules; a color pairing that technically passes contrast is not automatically an approved Rhapsody treatment.

  • Normal text: minimum contrast ratio 4.5:1.
  • Large text: minimum 3:1 (approximately 24px regular, or 18.5px bold and above).
  • UI components and meaningful graphic elements: minimum 3:1 against adjacent colors.
  • Decorative graphics: contrast requirements do not apply only when the element conveys no information.

Rhapsody Blue on navy

On navy (#0B2C47), the two blues split by size. Rhapsody Blue (#1A81F4) reads at 3.75:1—fine for large or heavy type, but below the 4.5:1 small body text needs. Light blue (#B4D8FF) reads at about 9.7:1 and stays legible at any size.

When to use which on navy

  • Rhapsody Blue on navy: large headlines and accent words (roughly 24px+, or ~19px bold), plus sizable graphics such as large bars, blocks, and big dots, where the blue stays vibrant.
  • Light blue on navy: body copy, captions, labels, secondary and accent lines, thin rules, small icons, and small dots—anything below the large-text threshold.
  • Rule of thumb: if it’s large enough to pass 3:1, Rhapsody Blue works; anything smaller needs 4.5:1, so switch to light blue—size alone won’t rescue small blue copy.

Teal in small applications

Small teal elements on light grounds keep failing contrast checks, and the common “fix”—darkening the teal—creates an off-brand second teal. The rule that prevents it: when a teal application fails contrast, change the ground or the role, never the hex. Teal (#23C5BF) reads at 6.7:1 on navy (passes at any size) and 2.1:1 on white (fails at any size), so the ground decides the role.

The rules

  • One teal. #23C5BF is the only teal. Never darken, tint, or theme-convert it to pass a contrast check — a “fixed” teal is an off-brand color.
  • Teal type lives on navy. Eyebrows, labels, actionlinks, and small icons may be teal on navy (6.7:1). This is the only ground where teal carries type.
  • On light grounds, teal is an accent, not a message. Use the teal tick (a 16×3px bar before a navy label), the module accent line, the teal seam, a pathway top edge, or structured dots. The text beside it carries the meaning in navy; the accent carries the color. Links stay Rhapsody Blue.
  • Meaningful teal graphics on light (a chart series, a status mark) need direct navy labels so nothing rides on the teal alone; add a thin navy keyline if the shape must read crisply at small sizes. Purely decorative teal (seams, ticks, dots, device glows) is exempt from contrast thresholds.
Teal is immutable

If a teal application fails an accessibility check, the hue is never the thing that changes. Move the element to navy, or demote the teal to an accent beside navy text. One teal, everywhere, always #23C5BF.
